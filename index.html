Where the numbers come from. ESPN's public men's D-I scoreboard feed — one poll serves the live table, Your day and the timeline. Heat, projections and break windows are models and are labelled as such.
Heat. 0 until 4:00 of the 2nd half — no crunch before the final media timeout. Then each point of margin costs 30 seconds of clock: a tie with 0:30 left is 1.00, 1 point with 2:00 left equals 4 points with 0:30 left (0.50), 9 points with 0:30 left is 0.10. Under 0:30 a one-possession game keeps its heat and anything bigger fades. Before the window the row shows Exp: the expected heat at 0:30 given the score, the line and the time left. Overtime is always crunch.
Channel doesn't change a game's value. A game only has to be on somewhere. Colours are the household's, as in football: orange over the air, green the YouTube TV package, purple dashed everything else. Untelevised games are hidden and never hold you.
Watch links. The channel chip is the link when the game's own payload carries a player URL — event links, competition links and the media objects on geoBroadcasts and broadcasts are all scanned, and the href decides, not the rel tag. A /watch/player deep link outranks a bare ESPN watch URL, which outranks a partner player URL. Where the payload has none, ESPN's public airings list is matched on both team names and a tip within three hours; hovering the chip says which. No player URL, no link — a link to something that is not the game costs more than an empty cell.
Be back at 2:00. The header pill, Your day and the yellow line on each timeline block all read the same estimate of when the clock will show 2:00 of the 2nd half (Settings can make it 4:00). Ranges are 90%, never start before now, and tighten as the moment nears.
The clock does not tick between refreshes. It shows the last value the feed reported; Δ is seconds since that game last moved.
Game length is a starting model. Not yet fitted to measured games; this browser learns from every game it watches reach 2:00 and finish.
